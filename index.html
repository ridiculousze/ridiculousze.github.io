Bio

Curriculum Vitae

I am a postdoctoral researcher at Institut Jean Nicod, École Normale Supérieure – PSL in Paris, working with Nicolas Baumard. I completed my Ph.D. in Cognitive Neuroscience at Beijing Normal University, advised by Yanchao Bi.

I study cognition at the intersection of brain mechanisms and cultural processes. Our brains build internal mental models (knowledge, beliefs, values) to make sense of the world. As we learn from one another and pass ideas on, these models can adapt to the social and ecological conditions we live in.

My current research focuses on two aspects of mental representation — how people organize conceptual knowledge and how they reason about what others know, believe, and intend (theory of mind). I ask how these mental representations vary across societies and change over time. I am also interested in combining neuroimaging with computational analyses of language and cultural materials to connect neural mechanisms with broader patterns of cognition and culture.

Below are a few directions that span my past and current research, as well as questions I am interested in exploring.

Brain ↔ Cognition ↔ Cultural processes

The evolution of semantic space across cultures and time

Semantic knowledge, our mental representation of concepts, plays a crucial role in cultural evolution, yet its cultural manifestations remain largely unexplored. As a product of the human brain, semantic knowledge should also be understood through neurocognitive models.

I combine neurocognitive models of semantic representation with cross-cultural research to study how semantic systems vary across languages and cultures. I also examine how these systems adapt to ecological and cultural changes.

Cognition ↔ Cultural processes

The cultural evolution of recursive mindreading

Why are readers drawn to stories about what one person thinks another person believes? In chapter 29 of Dream of the Red Chamber, Daiyu suspects that Baoyu is feigning distress because he fears she doubts his devotion. Following her reasoning means representing minds within minds.

Has fiction become increasingly concerned with such recursive representations over time? I study this historical trajectory to understand how a universal human capacity, theory of mind, finds different expression across cultures and historical periods.

Using a validated language-model pipeline, we traced mental embeddedness across English, French, and Chinese fiction. This nesting of attributed mental states increased in all three traditions, beyond simply describing mental states more often. Nested representations also appeared increasingly in cooperative contexts and were more frequent in periods of higher urbanization and income.

Double negation across cultures and time

Why do people sometimes say “not impossible” rather than “possible”? I am interested in how people interpret double negation and what motivates its use. When does it soften an affirmation, and when does it strengthen a speaker’s stance?

How do these uses vary across languages and historical periods? I would like to combine corpus analyses with behavioral experiments to investigate how linguistic experience and communicative contexts shape the interpretation and cultural use of double negation.

Concrete and abstract language in cultural transmission

Political ideas can be expressed through concrete examples or abstract appeals to values. Do these forms differ in how consistently people understand them, how widely they spread, and how their meanings change through transmission?

This project asks whether individual differences in word meanings can predict these collective outcomes. I aim to combine models of individual semantic representations with naturalistic data on political discourse and online sharing. My central question is whether concrete language supports more consistent interpretations across people, and how this semantic alignment shapes the spread and transformation of ideas.

Brain ↔ Cognition

Language experience as an organizing principle for semantic representation in the brain

Many accounts of semantic knowledge place sensory and motor experience at its foundation. But how does language contribute to what we know? The success of large language models highlights the potential of learning from statistical regularities in text. I ask whether such regularities also help organize semantic representations in the human brain.

We built graphs of word co-occurrence and used fMRI to test how their topological relations map onto neural semantic representations. Different brain systems were sensitive to different relations, including common neighbors and shortest paths. These effects were not explained by sensory-motor similarity, suggesting that language statistics help explain the organization of semantic knowledge in the brain.
